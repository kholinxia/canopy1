Identifying an ERT plant in the field

One of the biggest barriers to citizen science is confidence: many hikers see something unusual but aren't sure whether it's worth reporting. This page walks through a simple process for narrowing down what you've found, even without formal botanical training. The long-term goal of the Canopy project is to pair this manual process with an automatic visual identification engine, but this alpha release focuses on giving contributors a clear manual workflow first.

Start with the obvious visual cues

Before trying to match a tree to a species, look for a handful of large, easy-to-spot features, either on the tree itself or on what has fallen from it. A papery winged fruit, or a tall trunk that flares into buttress roots at the base, points to a dipterocarp such as Dipterocarpus or Hopea. A leaf made of several leaflets along one stalk, combined with fragrant resin on the bark, points to the incense-tree family and the genera Canarium and Santiria. Because their fruit, leaves and smell differ so clearly, this first step usually narrows things down quickly.

Narrowing down the species

Once you know the family, elevation and habitat are usually the fastest way to narrow things down further. A very tall tree with a straight, clean trunk and long-winged fruit on the ground in lowland forest could be Dipterocarpus grandiflorus or Dipterocarpus cornutus, while a tree in swamp forest that floods now and then may be Hopea nutans. For the incense-tree family, the resin-scented bark and leaflet stalks that dry black on Santiria laevigata, or the swampy habitat and reddish fruit of Santiria apiculata, are useful clues once you compare your photos against the species profiles on the Explore page.

  1. Identify the family from the fruit, leaves and bark of the tree.
  2. Note the elevation, habitat type, and nearby vegetation.
  3. Compare leaf shape, bark and fruit against the species pages.
  4. If still unsure, submit the sighting anyway and let a reviewer confirm it.
Elevation
Height above sea level; a strong indicator for narrowing down which Hopea or Santiria species you may have found.
Habitat type
Broad category such as lowland mixed dipterocarp forest, peat swamp forest, or freshwater swamp forest.

When in doubt, don't guess

It's always better to submit an uncertain sighting through the Contribute page than to skip reporting it. Reviewers on our team are able to confirm identification from good photographs even when the contributor isn't sure of the exact species.

Manual

How to identify a plant

  1. A winged fruit or buttressed trunk suggests a dipterocarp; compound leaves with fragrant resin suggest Burseraceae.
  2. Note the elevation and habitat.
  3. Compare leaves, bark and fruit with the Species page.
  4. Optional: choose a photo in the upload box and press Submit (preview only, no analysis yet).
  5. Still unsure? Go to Contribute.
