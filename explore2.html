Genera

Within each family, plants are grouped into genera whose members resemble each other closely. Canopy covers two genera for each of the two families on the Families page, so four genera in total: Dipterocarpus and Hopea from Dipterocarpaceae, and Canarium and Santiria from Burseraceae. All four appear in the PlantsData list for this project.

Dipterocarpus

Dipterocarpus is a genus of about 70 species of tall rainforest trees, found from Sri Lanka and India across Southeast Asia to the Philippines. Borneo holds the greatest variety, and many of its species grow nowhere else; D. cuspidatus, for example, is known from north-eastern Sarawak and D. sarawakensis is called the Sarawak keruing. The name comes from Greek and means “two-winged fruits”, a reminder that each fruit carries two long wings that help it spin away from the parent tree. The genus is a major source of timber and of a resinous oil sold as keruing or gurjun oil, and some species are also used in traditional herbal medicine.

Hopea

Hopea contains about 114 species and ranges from Sri Lanka and southern India across Myanmar and southern China to New Guinea. It is named after John Hope, the first Regius Keeper of the Royal Botanic Garden in Edinburgh. Most species are small to medium trees of the main and lower canopy in lowland rainforest, although a few, such as Hopea nutans, can grow tall enough to poke out above the forest roof. The low branches and sometimes thick buttress roots are good clues, and the smooth chocolate-brown bark of young trees later cracks and becomes mottled with grey.

Canarium

Canarium is a genus of about 120 species, native from tropical Africa through South and Southeast Asia and Malesia to Australia and the western Pacific. The trees are large evergreens that can reach 40 to 50 metres, with pinnate leaves, and individual trees are either male or female. People use the genus widely: Canarium indicum and the pili nut (C. ovatum) give edible nuts, C. luzonicum yields the resin known as elemi, and in Sarawak the fruit of Canarium odontophyllum, called dabai, is a celebrated seasonal delicacy. Linnaeus described the genus in 1754.

Santiria

Santiria was described by the botanist Carl Ludwig Blume and belongs to the incense-tree family. Its members are medium to large trees, often with buttress roots, and they carry leaves made of several leaflets arranged along one stalk. The flowers are tiny, with male and female flowers on separate trees, and the fruit is a drupe that is often lopsided and sits on a persistent calyx. Like its relatives, the genus has a strong resinous smell that people compare to turpentine or mango. In Malaysia the trees are known as kerantai or kedondong, and their wood is brown to yellow and of medium weight. Some varieties are restricted to Borneo, and one is found only in Sarawak.

Telling the four genera apart

  1. Decide the family first: winged fruit and buttressed trunks suggest Dipterocarpaceae, while compound leaves and fragrant resin suggest Burseraceae.
  2. Within the family, note the habitat and elevation where you found the tree.
  3. Compare your photographs with the species profiles on the Species page.

Field tip

Resin is a useful clue for both families. If a tree has clear or milky droplets on the bark, note their colour and photograph them, but never cut into the trunk to look for more.
