Species

Below are profiles for the eight species tracked in the Canopy alpha dataset: two for each of the four genera. Dipterocarpus and Hopea belong to the dipterocarps, while Canarium and Santiria belong to the incense-tree family. Each profile gives basic identification notes and the conservation status where one is known, to help contributors recognise the tree in the field.

Dipterocarpus grandiflorus

Dipterocarpus grandiflorus is a tall tree of lowland evergreen forest that can pass 40 metres. Its trunk is straight and round, clear of branches for up to 30 metres and as much as 135 cm across, and the grey or yellowish bark is only slightly fissured. The leaves are ovate and 10–18 cm long. After flowering, the calyx grows into the fruit, which carries five ridges and two long wings that can reach 22 cm. The tree ranges from the Andaman Islands and Myanmar through Thailand, Vietnam and Peninsular Malaysia to Sumatra, Borneo and the Philippines, usually below about 600 to 700 metres. It is assessed as Endangered because its forest habitat is shrinking, and it is a source of keruing timber and of the oleo-resin known as minyak keruing. Blanco described it in the second edition of Flora de Filipinas (1845).

Local name
Keruing belimbing (Peninsular Malaysia and Sabah); apitong (Philippines)
Status
Endangered (IUCN Red List).

Dipterocarpus cornutus

Dipterocarpus cornutus is a very large tree, reaching about 50 metres, with a tall trunk and bark that is paler than in most other keruing. Its leaves are among the biggest in the genus, 15 to 30 cm long and densely hairy underneath, and the pale yellow flowers are about 4 cm across. The fruit calyx is nearly round with five prominent knobs and two long wings of up to 21 cm. The species grows in Peninsular Malaysia, Singapore, Sumatra and southern and eastern Borneo, and is rated Least Concern. Its timber is sold as keruing, and the oil that flows from the trunk has been used as a varnish, for sealing boats and for lighting. The species was named by Dyer in the Flora of British India (1874).

Local names
Keruing gajah, keruing gombang; akas (East Kalimantan)
Status
Least Concern (IUCN Red List).
FamiliesGenera Enquire

Hopea nutans

Hopea nutans is a giant of the rainforest. The tallest tree measured so far stands at 82.8 metres in Tawau Hills National Park in Sabah. The species occurs in Peninsular Malaysia and Borneo, in lowland and hill mixed dipterocarp forest and in freshwater swamp forest, on sandy, limestone and ultrabasic soils that flood from time to time. It grows up to about 650 metres and is rated Near Threatened. Ridley named the species.

Status
Near Threatened.
FamiliesGenera Enquire

Hopea beccariana

Hopea beccariana can grow 45 metres tall, with a trunk up to 1.6 metres across, buttress roots and deeply fissured bark. Its leathery, ovate leaves are about 8 cm long, and each flower cluster carries up to five cream-coloured flowers. It grows in Thailand, Peninsular Malaysia, Sumatra and Borneo, in dipterocarp forest and on coastal hills between 150 and 1,200 metres. It is Vulnerable because land is converted to agriculture and trees are cut for timber. The name honours the Italian botanist Odoardo Beccari.

Local name
Merawan batu (in Singapore).
Status
Vulnerable (IUCN Red List).
FamiliesGenera Enquire

Canarium odontophyllum

Canarium odontophyllum is known as dabai in Sarawak and kembayau in Sabah and Brunei, and its fruit is sometimes called the Sarawak olive. The tree can reach 40 to 50 metres and is native to Borneo. The fruit has glossy black skin and yellow flesh around an elongated seed, and the seed is edible and is compared to almonds or pistachios. The fruit can be eaten raw or blanched, and the annual Pesta Dabai festival in Song celebrates it. Growers now propagate selected trees by budding and grafting. Miquel described the species.

FamiliesGenera Enquire

Canarium decumanum

Canarium decumanum is a very tall tree, up to 54 metres, with a trunk as wide as 150 cm and grey bark that is smooth to scaly. Its flowers are yellow-brown and its ellipsoid fruits grow up to 8.5 cm long. The species lives in lowland forest in Borneo, the Moluccas and New Guinea. Gaertner named it.

Height
Up to 54 m.
Fruit
Ellipsoid, up to 8.5 cm long.
FamiliesGenera Enquire

Santiria laevigata

Santiria laevigata is a medium to large evergreen tree, usually 15 to 30 metres tall and often buttressed. The flaking bark ranges from rusty red to fawn or grey and is strongly aromatic, and clear to creamy sticky resin oozes from the trunk. Each leaf has five to eleven leathery leaflets whose stalks are swollen at both ends and turn blackish when dry, and the resin-covered buds dry black and shiny. Male and female flowers grow on separate trees, and the fruit is a small drupe, flattened on one side, that ripens from yellow to red and finally black; it is edible. The species grows in Peninsular Malaysia, Sumatra, Borneo, Sulawesi, the Philippines and Singapore, in dry forest, freshwater swamp and lowland peat swamp forest up to about 1,200 metres or more. It is rated Least Concern overall but is Vulnerable in Singapore. Blume described it.

Local names
Kedondong, smooth-leafed santiria
Status
Least Concern (IUCN Red List); Vulnerable in Singapore.
FamiliesGenera Enquire

Santiria apiculata

Santiria apiculata is a smaller relative, usually 6 to 20 metres tall, though occasionally it reaches 40 metres, and it has short buttresses. The bark is grey or brown and smooth to flaky, and the pink-cream inner bark releases white drops of resin. Each leaf carries three to nine ovate to elliptic leaflets, and the whitish twigs and leaf stalks are a handy clue. The round to oval fruit is lopsided, about 1 to 2 cm long, and ripens from green through red to purplish-black. The tree is more often found in swampy ground than on dry land, normally at low altitude, and it occurs in Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines and Singapore. It is not considered threatened. A. W. Bennett named the species.

Local name
Pointed-leafed santiria
Status
Least Concern (IUCN Red List).
FamiliesGenera Enquire

Quick field checklist for any species

  1. Photograph the whole tree, the bark, and any fallen leaves or fruit.
  2. Note the GPS coordinates or a clear description of the location.
  3. Record the date and approximate time of observation.
  4. Avoid cutting or scraping the tree — observe only.

Why only eight species?

This alpha release intentionally covers a small, manageable dataset so the platform's design and workflow can be tested before it is expanded to the full range of ERT plants found in Sarawak.
