Families

A plant family brings together genera that share a common ancestor and a recognisable set of features. Canopy follows the PlantsData list supplied for this project, and the alpha dataset covers two families of rainforest trees that dominate the lowland and hill forests of Borneo: the dipterocarps (Dipterocarpaceae) and the incense-tree family (Burseraceae). Both are important for timber, resin or fruit, and both lose habitat wherever forest is cleared.

Dipterocarpaceae

Dipterocarpaceae is the great timber family of tropical Asia. Botanists count about 695 species in roughly 22 genera, and the name comes from the Greek for “two-winged fruit”, because many members drop seeds with papery wings that spin away from the parent tree. North Borneo, which covers Brunei, Sabah and Sarawak, is regarded as the richest place on Earth for dipterocarps. Many of these trees form the tallest layer of the forest, and they supply timber, plywood, resins and aromatic oils. That value is also the danger: heavy logging, illegal cutting and the conversion of forest to farmland have pushed many species towards extinction.

Described by
Carl Ludwig Blume, in 1825.
Distribution
Tropical forests from India and southern China through Malaysia and Indonesia to the Philippines, with Borneo the centre of diversity.
Conservation concern
Overcutting and habitat conversion leave a large share of Bornean species imperilled.

Burseraceae

Burseraceae is often called the incense-tree or torchwood family, because almost every part of its trees contains fragrant resin. Counts differ between studies, but the family holds around 17 to 19 genera and about 540 species, and the famous frankincense and myrrh both come from relatives of the Bornean trees. In Malesia the group known as the Canarieae, which includes Canarium, Dacryodes and Santiria, is especially well represented. The trees have flaking, aromatic bark and leaves divided into pairs of leaflets, they bear small flowers on separate male and female trees, and their fleshy fruits are eaten by birds and mammals that spread the seeds through the forest.

  1. Smell a fallen leaf or a flake of bark; Burseraceae often give off a sharp, resinous or almond-like scent.
  2. Look for compound leaves, where several leaflets sit along a single stalk with one at the tip.
  3. Check for sticky resin on the bark or on broken twigs, but avoid damaging the tree.

Why look at trees?

Big trees are easy to miss because their flowers and fruit are far overhead. Fallen fruit, leaves and bark flakes on the ground are often the best evidence, so photograph them next to something that shows their size, such as a coin or a hand.
